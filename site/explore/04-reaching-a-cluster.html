Reaching a Cluster

Every mark sits on its day's line, which is right, and it means marks a few minutes apart land on top of each other. In the journey view one pair shares an hour exactly; in the expense view fifteen groups share a price exactly. Three ways for the line to notice the cursor and let you reach in. Move along a row. Click a mark.

One

Spread

Only the group you are pointing at moves, and only sideways. Its members push apart along the line until each has room, keeping the group's centre where it was. Every other mark in the row holds still.

Costs: inside a group, position stops meaning the exact hour while you hover.

Two

Fan

The group lifts off the line into a short arc above it, in the order it already had, and drops back when you leave. The arc makes each member a separate target at a glance, and you can count them without reading anything.

Costs: marks leave the line, which is the thing that made the row readable, and an arc has to open sideways as well as up.

nothing reached yet
Three

Lens

No groups, no thresholds. The whole stretch of line under the cursor re-spaces, pushing outward from the point you are on and easing back to nothing at the edge of its reach. The mark under the cursor does not move, so the thing you are aiming at never runs away.

Costs: a wide neighbourhood is always in motion, not just the crowded bit.

These are real rows. Nara, Hiroshima and Shirakawa-go, at their real positions on the real 24-hour axis. Hiroshima holds two marks at the identical minute and a third less than two pixels off; Shirakawa-go has three inside fourteen.

Low fidelity on purpose: plain dots, no icons, no card, no photograph. The only question here is whether you can get at the mark you meant.
